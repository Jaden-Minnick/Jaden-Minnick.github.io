Why this research matters to me

From uranium chloride chemistry to integrated nuclear systems

I see molten salt chemical engineering as an enabling discipline for next-generation fission systems and potentially for fusion blankets. My immediate work is anchored in uranium chloride systems for fission, while my long-term mission includes responsible nuclear material recycling, efficient energy systems, and sustainable nuclear fuel cycles.

The transition from fundamental salt chemistry to deployable nuclear systems requires many additional steps, including materials engineering, safety analysis, fuel-cycle infrastructure, economic assessment, and collaboration across disciplines.

Primary Ph.D. project · Current

Uranium Chloride Molten Salts for Fission Reactors

I am currently investigating uranium chloride molten salt chemistry in the context of advanced fission reactors. Understanding local uranium–chloride coordination, atomic structure, and temperature-dependent chemical behavior is important for interpreting these systems and developing reliable reactor-relevant chemistry models.

Uranium chlorideFission reactor chemistryAtomic coordinationHigh-temperature salts
Expand: Scientific questions and approaches+

Research questions: How is uranium coordinated within chloride-based molten salts? How do changes in composition and temperature affect local structure and the interpretation of experimental observations? What can structural information contribute to models of fission fuel-salt chemistry?

Approaches: X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS/EXAFS), total-scattering and pair-distribution-function concepts, and atomistic modeling approaches such as reverse Monte Carlo analysis. The methods listed here describe the technical scope I am developing; particular data sets, measurements, and findings will be added when appropriate for public release.

Structure

Total scattering & pair distribution functions

Scattering measurements provide information about how atoms are distributed relative to one another. Pair distribution functions help characterize local distances and disorder, including in high-temperature systems where long-range crystal order may be absent.

Technical details +

The structure factor S(Q), measured in reciprocal space, can be transformed to a real-space pair distribution function G(r). Peak positions inform interatomic distances while widths and shapes can reflect disorder. Interpreting multicomponent molten salts may require atomistic models to separate overlapping contributions.

Research-group background: Ivanov presentation, pages 9–11. No new experimental result is claimed here.

Local chemistry

XAS, XANES & EXAFS

X-ray absorption spectroscopy helps investigate oxidation state and local coordination around selected elements, even in chemically complex systems.

Technical details +

XANES is sensitive to electronic and oxidation-state changes; EXAFS can constrain coordination shells, approximate neighbor distances, and local disorder through fitting of absorption oscillations. Combining XAS with structural simulations can strengthen interpretation.

Research-group background: Ivanov presentation, pages 17–18 and 21. Specific studies require independent validation and advisor approval before publication.
